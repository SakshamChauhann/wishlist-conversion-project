how this works

From unprompted wishlist complaints to a ranked opportunity list

Surveys tell you what people say when you ask a leading question. Reviews, forum posts and complaints tell you what people write unprompted — usually at the exact moment a saved item failed to become a purchase. This page reads that corpus with one fixed, published taxonomy, so what comes out the other end is a measurable ranking, not a set of impressions someone has to take on faith. Five things happen to get there, in order — click any of them to jump straight to that part of the page.

Collect the raw material

Public reviews, forum threads and complaints about wishlist and pre-purchase behaviour, sourced from the open web. Platform and source type are kept on every entry; no user identifiers.

Filter out the noise

Anything about delivery, payments, app bugs or riders gets dropped before coding starts — it's not about the decision to buy a saved item, so it can't answer this question.

Code it against a fixed scheme

Every remaining entry gets a theme, a blocking-strength rating and a shopper segment from one published taxonomy — the same rules for every row. Try it yourself on the Taxonomy tab.

Aggregate, don't eyeball

Frequency, severity-weighted volume, theme co-occurrence and segment breakdowns are all computed live from the corpus below on page load — nothing here is a hand-typed number.

Check the coder's own work

A sample is re-coded blind, from the raw text alone, and compared back against the original labels — agreement rate reported honestly, including the cases it got wrong.

why an engine, not a read-through

One fixed coding scheme applied to the whole corpus makes theme frequency and co-occurrence measurable and reproducible — by someone who disagrees with the conclusion — instead of a curated pile of quotes chosen to support a pre-existing opinion.

The business problem, stated plainly

Myntra already invests in fit confidence — a Size & Fit Intelligence engine covering an estimated 85% of eligible apparel, a "Maya" AI assistant reaching an estimated 90% of monthly active users, and a 30-minute quick-commerce line (M-Now) already at an estimated 10% of orders in live cities 🟡 company/PR-sourced, Aug 2026. None of that infrastructure is aimed at the specific moment this project targets: an item a shopper already wanted enough to save, that still doesn't convert. That's not a discovery problem (the shopper found it) or a delivery problem (Myntra ships fast) — it's the same confidence gap the fit-AI investment is meant to close, just left unclosed at exactly the step where the shopper is closest to buying.

The other half of the same gap shows up after purchase: independent industry research puts India fashion e-commerce return rates at roughly 25–40% against a ~10% all-category average, with 53–93% of those returns attributed to size/fit issues depending on methodology 🟡 GoKwik / Pragma / Coresight / PRIME AI, compiled 2026. A shopper who can't judge fit before adding to cart and a shopper who can't judge fit before checking out are the same shopper failing the same judgment call twice — which is why this project treats wishlist non-conversion and post-purchase returns as two readings of one underlying signal, not two separate problems.

Why not the two obvious alternatives. A better size-recommendation algorithm — Myntra already has one (the Size & Fit Intelligence engine, ~85% of eligible apparel per the estimate above); a marginally better version of an existing attribute-based prediction is a diminishing-returns bet, and it still can't answer the styling/social-validation quarter of the corpus, only the sizing quarter. Bigger or better-timed discounts — RICE-highest of all 10 options considered (Solutions tab, 6.25) and rejected anyway: this project's constraint rules it out, and independently, ~11 of the corpus's 16 price/availability entries are about an item disappearing mid-decision, not its price — a markdown can't un-sell stock that's already gone.

Hypotheses this project is built to test

Findings are worth more when they can fail. Each hypothesis below names the test that would prove it wrong, not just the result that would prove it right.

primary hypothesis · H1

If a wishlisted shopper gets an evidence-based fit/style confidence signal and an easy way to get a second opinion before buying, then ≥1-item-in-30-days wishlist conversion rises materially, because the corpus shows the barrier is judgment (fit, styling, social validation — 27 of 91 relevant entries, largely outside what Myntra's own fit-AI infrastructure already covers) rather than price or discovery for the addressable slice of the corpus.

Kill criterion: a pilot A/B (Ask Someone + Fit Twins visible vs. hidden, same catalogue and pricing) shows conversion uplift under 3 percentage points in the treatment cohort after one full cycle.
supporting hypothesis · H2

If a second opinion can be gotten inside the app in under a minute, then the number of shoppers who silently abandon a wishlisted item after a single moment of doubt falls, because the corpus shows the current workaround — screenshotting to a friend on WhatsApp — is already happening unprompted, just untracked and with no guaranteed reply.

Kill criterion: post-launch reply rate on Ask Someone requests falls below 40% at 24 hours, meaning the in-app loop is slower than the WhatsApp workaround it's meant to replace.
primary hypothesis · H3 — the transferability test

If the severity-weighted theme ranking from the discovery engine (fit, styling, social validation as the addressable cluster) is real rather than an artifact of which shoppers happen to post publicly and where, then the same ranking should reappear when the pipeline is re-run against Myntra's own first-party wishlist and return data, because the corpus is not just a convenience sample of complainers — it is a largely non-Indian one. Of 91 entries, only 7 carry any India-market signal and only 4 name Myntra; 20 come from a single UK parenting forum. The behaviour it documents (the closet confidante, the fit stall) is plausibly universal; the ranking of themes is the part that may not survive the trip to an Indian, occasion-driven, ethnic-wear-heavy catalogue.

Kill criterion: re-running the same taxonomy against first-party Indian data (Phase 0) produces a materially different top-2 theme ranking — in which case the MVP's targeting, not just its numbers, needs to be revisited. H3 is not optional and not "supporting": H1 rests on a theme ranking H3 is the only thing that validates, so Phase 0 gates Phase 1.

from here

The mechanism: outcome-based, not attribute-based

Myntra's existing Size & Fit Intelligence engine already predicts a size from body measurements — attribute-based prediction: given this body, this garment's spec, guess the size. That's a genuinely useful, already-built capability, and this project doesn't try to out-build it. Fit Twins is a different kind of signal: outcome-based. Every completed order already tells Myntra whether that specific shopper kept or returned it. Cluster shoppers by their own keep/return pattern across brands — no new data collection, no new consent, it's already sitting in the order and return tables — and for any item a new shopper wishlists, surface what shoppers with a similar pattern actually did with this exact SKU and size: "312 shoppers with a fit history like yours bought this in size M — 84% kept it." That's evidence a specific number of real people generated, not one model's confidence score.

The mechanism compounds on its own: every new completed order strengthens the read for the next wishlister of that SKU — a data moat that gets harder to copy the longer it runs, not easier. A style too new to have that data yet falls back to a Fit Jury state (the shopper's own order history fills the gap, honestly labeled as thinner evidence). Two different receipts do two different jobs, and collapsing them would break the mechanism: a Fit Receipt (one tap after delivery — kept or returned, and why) attaches a real outcome to a real SKU and size, and is the only thing that can grow a cohort or lift a style out of cold start; a Decision Receipt (one tap on a wishlist exit) is a non-purchase, so it can never yield a keep/return outcome — it is demand and diagnostic data, and the first wishlist-abandonment reason-code Myntra would have anywhere, directly feeding H3's re-run and Phase 0's instrumentation (Metrics tab). Both are implemented, not just described, in the "Second Look" MVP — try the blazer to see the cold-start state, then use the "not buying this" link to see a Decision Receipt captured live.

north star metric

30-Day Wishlist Conversion Rate

Share of wishlisting users who buy ≥1 wishlisted item within 30 days of saving it — the metric this entire project exists to move.

Unit of analysis is the user, matching the brief. Item-level rates stay diagnostic only: one user's several saved items are not independent observations, and analysing them as if they were would understate every standard error in the pilot.

Why this metric, not the obvious alternatives

A north star should survive someone trying to game it in either direction — inflate it cheaply, or hide a problem behind it. Three alternatives were considered and rejected first.

Wishlist-to-cart rateRejected — a shopper can be nudged into carting without ever resolving the judgment call this project targets. Inflatable without proving anything actually converted.
GMV from wishlisted itemsRejected — trivially inflated by nudging shoppers toward higher-priced wishlisted items regardless of fit confidence. Rewards revenue, not the judgment gap being tested.
Return-rate reductionRejected as the primary metric (kept as a Quality-layer check below) — lagging by weeks, and contaminated by return causes this project doesn't touch (damaged in transit, changed mind, wrong item shipped).

Why the chosen metric resists gaming in both directions: it can't be inflated by pushing low-confidence purchases through, because the guardrail (Low-Fit-Twins-score conversion rate, below) catches exactly that failure mode and gates Phase 2. And it can't be inflated by quietly suppressing the honesty of the signal either — hiding a low score to avoid discouraging a purchase would show up immediately as the guardrail moving the wrong way. Moving the north star without moving the guardrail against you is the only way to actually win on this metric.

The guardrail that decides whether this ships nationally. If showing a low Fit Twins score causes a shopper to abandon an item they would otherwise have bought — unknowingly, without ever seeing the alternative where the score stayed hidden — the honest conclusion is that transparency cost more than it earned, which is why R1 is the one risk this project treats as a reason not to ship rather than a thing to manage. That's why Phase 1 below is a controlled pilot A/B, not a feature flag, and why low-score conversion staying at ≥85% of the control group is a hard gate before national rollout. Phased rollout

PHASE 0 · INSTRUMENT — 4 weeks

Re-run this page's exact taxonomy against Myntra's own first-party wishlist and return data (H3's falsification test, R6's mitigation). Measure the current 30-day wishlist conversion baseline. Shadow-score Fit Twins against actual historical return outcomes without showing it to anyone. Ship Decision Receipt first and alone — it's the one piece of this MVP that is pure instrumentation, and it starts generating Myntra's first-ever wishlist-abandonment reason codes from week one. Nothing else customer-facing.

Gate to proceed: baseline is measurable; shadow Fit Twins scores correlate with real return outcomes at a meaningful level; first-party theme ranking doesn't materially contradict this corpus's ranking.

PHASE 1 · PILOT — 8 weeks, 2 categories (ethnic wear + dresses)

Ask Someone, Fit Twins and AI Style Read go live for a randomized cohort of eligible wishlist users in the two categories most concentrated in the fit/styling/social-validation cluster.

Gate to proceed: 30-day conversion uplift ≥3pp vs. control (H1); Ask Someone reply rate ≥40% at 24h (H2); low-score conversion ≥85% of control (guardrail).

Is 8 weeks / 2 categories actually enough — and what can't it detect?

A pilot that can't statistically distinguish its kill criterion from noise isn't a test, it's a coin flip with a nicer dashboard. Myntra discloses no wishlist-specific baseline conversion rate 🔴 not disclosed, so this uses a plausible baseline range from cross-industry saved-item benchmarks rather than picking a sample size after the fact to make the pilot look sufficient.

Unit of analysis: the wishlisting user, not the wishlisted item. The brief asks for the share of users who buy ≥1 wishlisted item in 30 days, so that is the randomisation and analysis unit everywhere in this project. Item-level rates are kept as diagnostics only — one user's several saved items are not independent observations, and treating them as though they were would understate every standard error on this page.

H1 — adequately powered. A two-proportion power calculation (80% power, α=0.05, two-sided) against the 3-point MDE in H1's kill criterion needs roughly 1,500–2,400 wishlisting users per arm across an assumed 8–15% baseline range — on the order of 3,000–4,800 users total. Two mid-traffic categories over 8 weeks clear that comfortably.

The guardrail is not powered at Phase 1 size, and saying otherwise would be the dishonest part. The guardrail is a subgroup test — it looks only at users shown a low Fit Twins score, perhaps 20–30% of an arm, so 400–600 users rather than 2,000. At that size, detecting the ≥85%-of-control rule (a 15% relative drop) at 80% power would require roughly 7,500 low-score users per arm — implying a total pilot of about 30,000–37,000 users per arm, some 15× larger than H1 needs. What 400–600 subgroup users can actually detect is a drop of roughly 50–65% relative. So the honest specification is two-stage: at Phase 1 the guardrail is a circuit breaker, able to catch a collapse and nothing subtler, and the ≥85% rule becomes a properly powered clearance test only at Phase 2's full-category scale. Phase 1 additionally tracks two better-powered proxies for the same failure — return rate on converted items, and low-score view→exit rate, both of which have far more events per user than conversions do.
PHASE 2 · SCALE A/B — 10 weeks, full category set

Expand to all categories, run as a true randomized control at scale rather than a single cohort.

Gate to proceed: north-star uplift holds at scale; guardrail still passes; post-purchase return rate on converted items doesn't materially worsen (confirms conversion isn't just moving the same failure downstream).

PHASE 3 · NATIONAL — 16 weeks, staged

Full rollout of the MVP, with Stock & Price Alert, Comparison Resolver and Intent Triage added incrementally, each gated by its own smaller pilot rather than shipped alongside the main rollout.
