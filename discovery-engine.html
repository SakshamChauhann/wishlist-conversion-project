how this works

From unprompted wishlist complaints to a ranked opportunity list

Surveys tell you what people say when you ask a leading question. Reviews, forum posts and complaints tell you what people write unprompted — usually at the exact moment a saved item failed to become a purchase. This page reads that corpus with one fixed, published taxonomy, so what comes out the other end is a measurable ranking, not a set of impressions someone has to take on faith. Five things happen to get there, in order — click any of them to jump straight to that part of the page.

Collect the raw material

Public reviews, forum threads and complaints about wishlist and pre-purchase behaviour, sourced from the open web. Platform and source type are kept on every entry; no user identifiers.

Filter out the noise

Anything about delivery, payments, app bugs or riders gets dropped before coding starts — it's not about the decision to buy a saved item, so it can't answer this question.

Code it against a fixed scheme

Every remaining entry gets a theme, a blocking-strength rating and a shopper segment from one published taxonomy — the same rules for every row. Try it yourself on the Taxonomy tab.

Aggregate, don't eyeball

Frequency, severity-weighted volume, theme co-occurrence and segment breakdowns are all computed live from the corpus below on page load — nothing here is a hand-typed number.

Check the coder's own work

A sample is re-coded blind, from the raw text alone, and compared back against the original labels — agreement rate reported honestly, including the cases it got wrong.

why an engine, not a read-through

One fixed coding scheme applied to the whole corpus makes theme frequency and co-occurrence measurable and reproducible — by someone who disagrees with the conclusion — instead of a curated pile of quotes chosen to support a pre-existing opinion.

Audit

A stratified ~10% sample (at least one item per theme) was pulled at random, stripped of its assigned label, and re-coded from scratch against only the taxonomy rules above — then compared back to the original label. This is what makes the theme counts falsifiable rather than asserted.

Honest read of this number: the sample mixes real quotes (public forum posts and reviews, exactly as messy as that implies) and synthetic entries written to fill thin themes — and the agreement rate held up across both, which is a better signal than a demonstration corpus alone would give. It's still a page-embedded sample, not live production-scale review data, so treat this as proof the taxonomy is codeable and reasonably stable, not a guarantee of what agreement looks like at full scale.
